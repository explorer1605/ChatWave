Maybe we'll only know after the fact when the economic history of our era is being written. Now, with that out of the way, let's look at the big tax changes this year. To begin with, personal income tax rates themselves are unchanged from last year. What has changed though is the legal backbone of the tax system. From 1st April 2026, India will move to a new income tax act, replacing the old 1961 law. A large chunk of this year's tax amendments are technical cleanups like adding missing definitions, correcting section references that pointed to the wrong clauses, and tightening languages. So, the new act behaves the way it was meant to. Where this budget does make its presence felt, however, is in the capital markets, especially derivatives. The market was clearly unhappy. Every time you trade certain securities on an exchange, you pay a security transaction tax or STD. This year, the government has raised STD across futures and options in a move that echoes what it did just 2 years ago. Here's how the new ST rule works. If you sell a future, the tax you pay is now increased to 0.05% from 0.02%, 02% 150% increase. The tax on selling options has gone up from 0.1% to 0.15% of the premium, a 50% increase. If you exercise an option at expiry, the STD is going to be 0.15% from 0.125%. The distinction between selling and exercising an option is important here. When you sell an option, what's being traded is the premium, the upfront price of the option contract. The SGT in that case is charged on the premium amount. When an option is exercised at the expiry, on the other hand, the premium is no longer relevant. What matters then is the option's intrinsic value, how much it is in the money. So the ST on exercised options is charged on that intrinsic value instead. In effect, the budget raises the cost of trading across the board, making high turnover derivatives more expensive at the margin. The next change that has happened is in how share buybacks are taxed. Since the 2024 budget, buyback proceeds were treated in a slightly awkward way. Beginning with that year, the cash you received from a buyback was taxed like your dividend income at income tax liab rates. Meanwhile, the cost of your shares sat separately as capital loss. This capital loss could be adjusted against any capital gains. This was a very convoluted system and the new budget simplifies that treatment. Buyback proceeds are now taxed as capital gains. You take what you received, subtract what you paid for the shares and tax the difference. There is however a clear distinction for a company's promoters because they get a say in whether and when a buyback happens opening the doors to misuse and so the budget introduces an additional tax layer for them and so promoter gains from buybacks are taxed at an effective of 22% for corporate promoters and 30% for non-corporate promoters. For ordinary shareholders, the new change is mostly for clarity, but for promoters, it is also about limiting tax arbitrage. Then there are changes to how sovereign gold bonds or SGBS will be taxed. They continue to enjoy tax-free capital gains on redemption as they were before. But the budget makes one very important clarification which effectively reduces who can avail that benefit. The exemption is now explicitly limited to investors who subscribed to the bond at the time of original issuance and held it continuously until maturity. So if you buy an SGB from the secondary market and later redeem it, your gains will be taxed like any normal capital gains. Now onto money going out of the country. The budget tries to lower the friction on outward remittances. To be clear, it doesn't change the final tax burden. It reduces how much tax will be collected at the source when a transfer is being made. TCS rates have been reduced to 2% for education and medical remittances. Overseas tour packages too are now subject to a flat 2% TCS irrespective of the amount. Since TCS is usually adjustable against your final tax liability, this mostly affects cash flow rather than your tax burden. Less money gets locked up up front when you pay fees or book travel abroad. Finally, on the indirect tax side, there's a big consumerf facing relief. The tariff rate on dutyable goods imported for personal use has been reduced from 20% to 10%. This applies to individuals bringing goods for personal consumption. This reflects a recognition that not all imports are commercial or deserving of a high duty wall. Finally, let's come to the big policy announcements from the finance minister speech that got us excited. In our first takeaway, we got a sense of what kind of manufacturing India wants to prioritize. Take electronics, a huge winner in this budget. The government is trying to move from mere assembly of devices to making the parts that go into phones like capacitors, camera lenses, PCBs, etc. That shows up in how its allocation of older PLIs have reduced significantly from last year's budget as those schemes have matured. At the same time, the outlay for electronic components have been raised by 75% to 40,000 cr rupees. With the advent of AI, India has also made allocations to high-tech sectors. For instance, the budget makes room worth 8,000 cr to develop semiconductors. The budget also provides tax holidays to foreign companies setting up data centers in India. Pharma is yet another bright spot. The government launched Biofarma Shaki, a 10,000 cr scheme to promote biologics and biosimilars. On the daily brief, we've covered how the biologics are far more complex than generics we're known for exporting. Much like electronics and data centers, making biologics is a very capitalintensive process. In contrast though, labor intensive sectors like textiles and apparel have slipped down the priority list. For instance, while the budget has announced many schemes to promote textiles, this year's capital outlay for the Ministry of Textiles has remained somewhat flat compared to last year. Moreover, the PLI scheme for textiles has also had lackluster performance. For many countries, making textiles has been a stepping stone to largecale economic development. While still quite globally competitive, Indian textiles lag behind countries like China, Vietnam and Bangladesh. Prioritizing capital intensive sectors usually leads to economic growth with slower employment generation. Our second takeaway is about how the Indian government is trying to convince private investors to follow India's public capex. As we've mentioned above, the government has in recent years made capital spending a priority. One of its biggest new projects is the announcement of seven high-speed rail projects connecting metros and tier one cities. Such capeex aims to absorb early risks in long-term infrastructure projects so that private capex feels comfortable chipping in. However, as we've covered before multiple times, the private sector has responded quite sluggishly to government nudges. So the budget has introduced new mechanism to make these projects more investable. One such tool is a total return swap. See, corporate bonds are often used to raise funds for long-term projects. But investors sometimes don't like them because of their long time horizon and risks. The TRS allows the investors to gain or hedge exposure to corporate bonds without directly owning the bond. India will also improve investor access to derivatives based on the corporate bond market. The budget also created infrastructure risk guarantee fund. It aims to provide partial guarantees to lenders of infrastructure and construction loans. This would help in cushioning early stage construction risks for private developers. Taken together, these measures signal an attempt to shift risk away from banks and towards capital markets. Bank deposits have much shorter maturities than 10 20-year infrastructure projects, making banks ills suited to carry such risks at scales. Corporate bonds, however, are better suited to infrastructure financing as the maturity periods match. The budget treats logistics and transport as a binding constraint on our manufacturing and exports. Our costs of transporting goods and minerals both within and outside our borders are higher than our peers like China and Vietnam and all sorts of things can become a choke point. A few years ago, for instance, the world suffered from an acute shortage of shipping containers that slowed global trade. The budget's large infrastructure push aims in part to solve these bottlenecks. For instance, the government announced a new dedicated freight corridor connecting Dankani in West Bengal to Surat, Gujarat. This corridor will cover India's east west cross-section connecting mineral-rich areas to industrial hubs in a very cost-ffective manner. At the moment, India has two functional corridors and three more planned ones. This has contributed to the increase in railway spending. As per the national rail plan, the government aims to increase the model share of rail in India's total freight movement to 45% by 2030. Another important scheme is the new 10,000 cr outlay to manufacture own containers. As India signs a flurry of trade deals with other countries, we'll need to have plenty of our own container capacity. Lastly, the budget makes plenty of room for rare earth and critical minerals. The government is creating four rare earth corridors that span four states. That's Odisha, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and Andhra Pradesh. These states were chosen in particular because of their long coastlines. After all, critical minerals are often found in monazite sands that line the beaches of peninsula India. However, they're often found alongside a radioactive material called thorium, which the government guards strongly because of its atomic energy potential. Now, could these special corridors help ring fence this hazard? We don't know. Perhaps this is also connected to our logistics push. The budget's plan for new rail freight corridors and inland waterways often involve Odisha, which houses many of these minerals. But beyond that, the government has also introduced new incentives to encourage participation in minerals that we need for making EVs, solar panels and other new age technologies. Some of them are as follows. The basic custom duty on monide has been reduced to zero. This budget also includes critical minerals in schedule 12 of the income tax act which allows companies to claim tax deduction on expenses incurred during exploration and mining. The custom duty on sodium antimonate, a key raw material used in making solar glass is proposed to be removed. But as we've covered before, mining in India isn't difficult just because the incentives aren't big enough. Those problems, it seems, haven't yet been addressed in the budget.
